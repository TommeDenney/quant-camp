## Lecture 05 — Time series, stochastic process, autocorrelation

Objectives (s.14): time-series concept; "stationarity and the difference between time-domain and frequency-domain analysis"; "Define the autocovariance and autocorrelation functions and describe how they are related"; "Explain how serial correlation affects statistical inference"; "Quantify the impact through estimating the effective sample size (N_eff)"; "Account for autocorrelation when conducting hypothesis tests".

### Definitions
- s.15 **Time series**: "A sequence of data points collected or recorded at successive points in time"; "Temporal ordering matters: the data points may not be independent and often share common behaviors, such as persistence."
- s.16 **Stationarity**: "Past and future values of a time series are statistically similar; that is, the process that generates the time series is stationary." "We often analyze time series assuming the data are stationary, but most real data are not"; can process the data so stationarity is reasonable — e.g. temperature diurnal cycle and Jan vs Jul differences: use daily or annual averages.
- s.17 **Time domain**: analyse the series as a function of time, "characterizing the process through statistics computed over time lags". **Frequency domain**: in terms of the frequencies (timescales/periods) that make up its variability. "In either domain, a time-series model is often used to characterize the properties of the data mathematically."
- s.18 **Stochastic process**: "a random function that generates a collection of random variables indexed by a parameter, such as time or space"; "described by its moments (e.g., mean and variance, which might depend on time)"; time-series data = observations sequenced in time $X_1,\dots,X_N$; written
$$X_t=\mu_t+\epsilon_t\quad(\mu_t:\text{ a deterministic function of time},\ \epsilon_t:\text{ white noise}\sim N(0,\sigma^2)\text{ iid})$$
- s.19 deterministic term depends on hypotheses about mechanisms: diurnal cycle, seasonality, intraseasonal (MJO), interannual (ENSO), decadal (AMO, PDO), trend (climate change). s.20 AMO, s.21 PDO maps/indices.
- s.22 modelling μ_t: long-term trend → μ_t as smoothed time variation; natural variability like ENSO → remove climatology: $X'_t=X_t-\hat\mu_t$ (the anomaly; "this is how you get Nino3.4a"). Figures: Niño 3.4 raw vs seasonal cycle removed.
- s.23 earlier tests "assumed our data were independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.)"; "Many climate and weather features have **persistence** … data are often correlated over sufficiently short spatial and temporal scales" (NYC today/tomorrow; NYC vs Westchester).
- s.24/29 why care: predictive power; biases in hypothesis testing — tests assume independent observations; "more data points → more independent pieces of information → the estimated standard error shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$ → your test becomes more confident"; "**When your data are autocorrelated, each new observation carries less new information than an independent one would**" (SST example: ocean heat content/thermocline hasn't changed in a month); "One can take into account the dependence … by adjusting the sample size: the effective sample size (N_eff)".

### Autocovariance / ACF (s.26–28)
$$\hat c(\tau)=\frac1N\sum_{t=1}^{N-|\tau|}(X_{t+|\tau|}-\hat\mu_{t+|\tau|})(X_t-\hat\mu_t),\qquad \hat c(0)=\frac1N\sum (X_t-\hat\mu_t)^2=\sigma_x^2$$
"Similar to the covariance for X and Y, but now Y is just X at another time." Examples τ=1,2,N−1 written out.
$$\hat\rho(\tau)=\frac{\hat c(\tau)}{\hat c(0)}\quad(\text{autocovariance divided by the variance})$$
"c is symmetric about τ=0"; "$0\le\hat\rho\le1$" (sic, see errata); "If $X_t$ is not periodic, then ρ→0 as τ→∞".
- s.28 lag scatter (synthetic AR(1) φ=0.7, n=200): lag1 r=0.649, lag2 0.426, lag5 0.214, lag10 0.003; sample ACF ρ̂(1)=0.648. "Slightly different from the Pearson sample correlation; however, when n>>k, they are quite similar". CI (Bartlett) $=1.96/\sqrt N=1.96/\sqrt{200}=0.139$.

### Effective sample size (s.30, 32–39)
$$N_{eff}(1)\approx N\times\frac{1-\rho(1)}{1+\rho(1)}$$
"This may change depending on the what test you are looking at." "with a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.7, the effective sample size is only ~17% of N (!), which inflates the confidence interval" (verified 0.176). "Using sample ρ̂(1)=0.648, N_eff = 42.9" (verified 42.7 at 0.648); CI using N_eff: $1.96/\sqrt{42.9}=\pm0.299$ vs ±0.139 with n=200. "For white noise ρ̂(1)=0 → N_eff = N"; "As ρ̂(1) increases, N_eff decreases".
- s.33 white noise n=200: lag-1 r=−0.052; ACF inside ±CI.
- s.34–35 NOAA **RONI** (Relative Oceanic Niño Index) 1950–2026: tasks — autocovariance & ACF lags 1–10 months, effective N, 95% CI. Result figure: naive CI (n=916) ±0.0648; effective-N CI (n_eff=15.3, using ρ1=0.967) ±0.5013 (verified). ACF crosses zero ~lag 10 months, negative at 12–24.
- s.36 RONI mean shift 1951–1980 vs 1996–2025 with AR(1) $X_t=0.97X_{t-1}+\epsilon_t$, $t=\frac{\hat\mu_2-\hat\mu_1}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt N}$: naive observed t = −5.81 (theoretical df=718) vs corrected t = −0.78 (df≈12.4) — significance disappears once N_eff is used.
- s.37–39 hands-on NYC Tmax anomaly (seasonality removed) 2000–2005: find lag where persistence is lost (95% CI); one-sample t-test $t=\frac{\hat\mu_t-0}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt N}$. Results: naive CI (n=2192) ±0.0419; N_eff=507.5 (ρ1=0.624) ±0.0870 (verified); persistence lost by ~lag 12–13 days. Naive t=−5.44 (df=2191) vs corrected t=−2.62 (df=509.2). "Both tests reject H0, i.e., the mean in the NYC temperature anomaly (−1.07F) deviates significantly from 0, but the p-value differs depending on whether the effective N is used"; "The chance of type I error reduced when considering effective N".

## Lecture 06 — AR models and filtering

### AR models (s.42–58)
- s.43 "An autoregressive (AR) model represents the value at one time step as a linear combination of values at previous time steps, plus white noise." "AR models have the useful property of generating data with prescribed values of ACF (φ)."
$$X_{t+\Delta t}=\phi X_t+W_{t+\Delta t}+k\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X_t=\phi X_{t-\Delta t}+W_t+k$$
(labels: what you want to predict with lead time Δt; X at time t; white noise; errors/constant).
- s.44 AR(1): $X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+W_t+k$; "$W_t$ is Gaussian white noise with variance of $\phi_w^2$" (sic: σ_w²); "k is a constant"; at t=0 initial value $X_0$ (0 variance); $X_1=\phi X_0+k+W_1$; "The **expected value of $X_1$ is $\phi X_0+k$** with **variance $\sigma_w^2$**, so $X_1$ has a Gaussian distribution."
- s.45 derivation:
$$X_2=\phi^2x_0+(1+\phi)k+(W_2+\phi W_1),\quad X_t=\phi^tx_0+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\phi^jk+\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\phi^jW_{t-j}$$
$$p(x_t|x_0)\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\phi^tx_0+k\frac{1-\phi^t}{1-\phi},\ \sigma_W^2\frac{1-\phi^{2t}}{1-\phi^2}\right)$$
"As t→∞, assuming |φ|<1, the distribution converges to $\sim N\!\left(k\frac{1}{1-\phi},\ \sigma_w^2\frac{1}{1-\phi^2}\right)$." Figure: p(y) at t=2, 10, 100, asymptotic — the forecast distribution widens and its mean relaxes toward the process mean.
- s.46–47 AR(1) for ENSO, lead 1 month: "Monthly Niño 3.4 anomaly with 1 month lag is φ̂ 0.967"; $\hat X_t=0.967X_{t-1}$; residuals $W_t=X_t-\hat X_t$, then $\sigma_{\hat W_t}$. "Often we apply ordinary least squares to fit φ and k, but since we have φ, and for this data we can set k to 0."
- s.48 "Is AR(1) a good model for ENSO?" — compare modeled (theoretical) and observed ACF (Lecture06_examples.ipynb).
- s.49 lead 1–12 months: $p(x_t|x_0)\sim N\!\left(\phi^tx_0,\ \sigma_w^2\frac{1-\phi^{2t}}{1-\phi}\right)$ (sic denominator); forecasts from 1990-07 and 1997 (1997–98 El Niño missed: forecast decays while observation grows).
- s.50 "Predicted Niño 3.4 did not capture the observed Niño 3.4's characteristics, as shown in the ACF figure": AR(1) theoretical ACF $\phi^k$ (φ=0.967) stays ~0.45 at lag 24, observed ACF hits 0 at ~10 months and goes negative.
- s.51 spring predictability barrier (Torrence & Webster 1998, autocorrelation of Niño 3 vs initial month and lag): "AR(1) will not be good for anything that has periodic variability, such as annual cycle." "An idea is that one could develop a **periodic** one, i.e., build φ(t) as a function of initial month, instead of using a single autocorrelation for all months."
- s.52 periodic AR(1) (correlation-based) vs observed persistence for starting months Jan/Apr/Jul/Oct: periodic model still over-persists.
- s.53 AR(2): $X_t=\phi_1X_{t-1}+\phi_2X_{t-2}+W_t+k$; "**φ₂ is not the lag-2 autocorrelation coefficient, but the partial effect of $X_{t-2}$ on $X_t$ after accounting for $X_{t-1}$**." AR(2) forecasts from 1990-07, 1997.
- s.54 RONI: AR(2) theoretical ACF matches observed (crosses zero ~lag 10, negative lobe) far better than AR(1).
- s.55 12-month forecast from April 1997 (into 1997–98 El Niño): standard AR(1), periodic AR(1), standard AR(2), periodic AR(2) with 95% CI — periodic AR(2) closest but still underestimates.
- s.56–57 hands-on AR(1) for NYC Tmax anomaly: forecast from 2000-01-01, 10-day lead, 95% forecast interval; ACF naive vs N_eff. "**0.6291 → A lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.6291 explains only ~40% of the variance**" (r²=0.396 ✓).
- s.58 "In practice, AR models are usually used together with exogenous predictors as part of a linear regression model - AutoRegressive with eXogenous inputs (**ARX**)"; TC intensity example $V_{t+12h}-V_t=L(V_t,V_{t-12h},X_t,X_{t+12h})+\varepsilon_{t+12h}$ (L linear regression, ε stochastic forcing). s.60 AR intensity model performance for 2025 hurricane season.

### Filtering (s.61–70; repeated/expanded in Lecture 07 — see L07-08 notes)
- "A technique that can be used to remove high-frequency noise or low-frequency variability from a time series and isolate the frequencies of interest."
  - **low-pass**: "retains only the low-frequency variability (smoothing, average, etc.)"; **high-pass**: "retains only high-frequency variability"; **band-pass**: "retains a 'band' of frequencies".
- High-pass = raw − low-pass: `Xhypass = x – x_lowpass`; "Monthly: raw – 12yr running mean" (sic — L07 corrects to 12-month); "Interdecadal: raw – 10yr running mean"; "Detrend: raw − trend".
- Band-pass: subtract one low-pass from another with a higher-frequency cutoff: `X2-7 year: x-2yr_running_mean – X_7yr running_mean`.
- s.66 deriving/filtering out the long-term trend (detrended series). s.70 hands-on: has NYC Tmax increased since 1999 in January? July?
