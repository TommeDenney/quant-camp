## AR(1) coding example (p.7–9)
- p.8 $X_t=\phi X_{t-1}+W_t+k$; $\hat X_t=\rho X_{t-1}\to W_t;\ \sigma_{\hat W_t}$; $p(x_t|x_0)\sim N\!\left(\phi^tx_0,\ \sigma_w^2\frac{1-\phi^{2t}}{1-\phi}\right)$ (sic — code uses $1-\phi^2$).
  Code (viewed):
  ```python
  max_lag = 1
  x = df.anomaly.values
  rho = stattools.acf(x, nlags=max_lag, adjusted=False)[1]
  x_hat = rho * x[:-1]
  residuals = x[1:] - x_hat
  sigma_w_hat = residuals.std()
  # 3. Forecast: start Jan 1, lead time = 10 days
  idx0 = np.where(df["DATE"] == "2000-01-01")[0][0]   # locating index for initial time
  x0 = x[idx0]
  forecast_horizon = 10
  t_ahead = np.arange(0, forecast_horizon + 1)
  x_hat_mean = (rho**t_ahead) * x0
  x_hat_var = sigma_w_hat**2 * (1 - phi_hat**(2*t_ahead)) / (1 - phi_hat**2)
  x_hat_var[0] = 0
  x_hat_std = np.sqrt(x_hat_var)
  plt.fill_between(..., x_hat_mean - 1.96*x_hat_std, x_hat_mean + 1.96*x_hat_std, label='95% forecast interval')
  ```
  (Inconsistency: variable `rho` vs `phi_hat` used in the same cell.)

### Linear trend + CI (p.15–20)
- p.15 linear regression on time:
$$X_t=\beta_0+\beta_1t+\varepsilon_t$$
  (time series data; intercept; "Trend (slope) – the quantity of interest, so pay attention to its units"; noise/regression error). Fitted trend $\hat X_t=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1t$ (fitted values). **Detrended series** $X'_t=X_t-\hat X_t$.
- p.16 CI for $\hat\beta_1$: "Under the standard linear regression assumptions (ε_t is independent), the slope estimator β₁ is itself normally distributed, so we can apply the t-distribution"; "The standard error is computed from the residuals, scaled by the spread of the predictor (time)":
$$SE(\hat\beta_1)=\frac{\sigma_{X_t-\hat X_t}}{\sqrt{\sum(t_i-\bar t)^2}}=\frac{\sqrt{\sum\hat\varepsilon^2/(n-2)}}{\sqrt{\sum(t_i-\bar t)^2}}$$
  "Here we use n−2 because it is already defined that mean of ε_t should be 0 and ε_t is independent to t". α=0.05, two-sided:
$$\hat\beta_1\pm t_{\alpha/2}\times SE(\hat\beta_1);\qquad \hat\beta_0+(\hat\beta_1\pm t_{\alpha/2}SE(\hat\beta_1))\times t=\hat X_t\pm(t_{\alpha/2}SE(\hat\beta_1))\times t\approx\hat X_t\pm(t_{\alpha/2}SE(\hat\beta_1))\times t_{max}$$
- p.17 autocorrelated residuals:
$$SE_{eff}(\hat\beta_1)=SE(\hat\beta_1)\times\sqrt{\frac{N}{N_{eff}}}$$
  "Here we do not need to change the SE(β̂₁); the impact of N_eff on df will be taken care by the scale $\sqrt{N/N_{eff}}$"; "$t_{\alpha/2}$ also need to use the new df".
- p.18–19 GISTEMP 1990–2025 trend: fitted trend **0.2353 °C/decade**; "**warming rate range 95%CI: [0.0204, 0.026709] °C/year**" (≈0.204–0.267 °C/decade); constant-width bands ±0.050 and ±0.114 °C "(from β 95% CI at max t)". Lecture07_example.ipynb.

### Seasonal cycle, anomalies, climatology (p.21–31)
- p.21 "One feature of many climate and geophysical variables that results in autocorrelation is the **seasonal cycle**. Reducing autocorrelation may not be the only reason to remove the seasonal cycle, but it is often the primary one." Figure: NYC Tmax ACF raw (stays ~0.6–0.8 out to lag 30, N_eff=98.6 with ρ1=0.914, CI ±0.1974) vs anomaly (decays by ~lag 13).
- p.22 "Another reason is when the seasonal cycle dominates the raw series visually and statistically. Any interannual+ signal you actually care about … is tiny by comparison and gets visually and statistically swamped unless you remove the seasonal cycle first." Figure: NYC TMAX trend with seasonality 0.872 °F/decade, R²=0.002, p=0.4321.
- p.23 "Anomaly is defined as the deviation from time mean": $X'_i(t)=X_i(t)-\bar X$; "The time mean is not that meaningful when we have a large seasonal cycle"; "Define anomalies instead by subtracting the seasonal time mean for each day or month of the year (how we get Nino3.4 anomaly)":
$$X'_{i,j}(t)=X_{i,j}(t)-\bar X_j,\quad \bar X_j=\text{climatological mean of X during season/month } j$$
  "One can then conduct various analysis (e.g., trend analysis) with anomalies".
- p.24–26 NYC monthly Tmax 1999–2026: $\bar X_{July}$ = 85.2 °F, $\bar X_{Feb}$/Jan mean = 39.5 °F; climatology Jan 39.5, Feb 42.6, Mar 51.2, Apr 62.5, May 71.5, Jun 79.9, Jul 85.2, Aug 83.4, Sep 76.7, Oct 64.9, Nov 54.6, Dec 44.6 °F (±1 std band). Decomposition: $X_{i,j}(t)$ = repeated $\bar X_j$ + $X'_{i,j}(t)$.
- p.27 running-mean "are filters that low-pass filter the data by averaging over specific time interval of interest":
$$L=3:\ y_t=\frac{x_{t-1}+x_t+x_{t+1}}{3};\qquad L=6:\ y_t=\frac16\left(\tfrac12x_{t-3}+x_{t-2}+x_{t-1}+x_t+x_{t+1}+x_{t+2}+\tfrac12x_{t+3}\right)$$
  "For removing seasonality, we can set L = 12 for monthly data or L = 31 for daily data"; "This is considered as running-trend". p.28 general forms:
$$\text{even }N:\ \bar x_i=\frac1N\left[\tfrac12x_{i-h}+\sum_{k=-h+1}^{h-1}x_{i+k}+\tfrac12x_{i+h}\right],\ h=\lfloor N/2\rfloor;\qquad \text{odd }N:\ \bar x_i=\frac1N\sum_{k=-h}^{h}x_{i+k},\ h=\lfloor (N-1)/2\rfloor;\quad h\le i\le n-h-1$$
- p.29 decomposition via running mean: "Low-frequency → from running mean (for interannual, decadal, and trend)"; "High-frequency → from the residual (for seasonal or intra-seasonal)";
$$X_{i,j}(t)=X_{run\_trend}(t)+X'_{i\_run}(t),\quad X'_i(t)=\bar X_{j\_run}(t)+X'_{i,j\_run}(t)$$
  Steps: (1) $X_{run\_trend}$ = running mean L=12 on monthly data; (2) $X'_{i\_run}=X(t)-X_{run\_trend}$, seasonality $\bar X_{j\_run}$ = mean of $X'_{i\_run}$ per month j; (3) residual $X'_{i,j\_run}$.
- p.30 direct climatology vs running-mean-based seasonality: nearly identical (NYC, seasonal deviation −23 °F Jan to +22 °F Jul).
- p.32 hands-on (Oregon monthly temperature): (1) climatology seasonal cycle; (2) long-term trend from deseasonalized data; (3) trend after removing interannual variability shorter than 7 years. p.33 Jong et al. 2023 7-year running mean.

## Lecture 08 — Spectral analysis (p.35–58)
- p.36 time vs frequency domain (same definitions as L05).
- p.37 "Analysis in the frequency domain involves representing a data series in terms of contributions made at different timescales" — e.g. period 24 h or a year (8760 h), frequencies 1/24 and 1/8760. "**Harmonic analysis** represents the fluctuations or variations in a time series as having arisen from the addition of a series of sine and cosine functions." "**The Power spectrum** quantifies how much of a time series' total variance is explained by each frequency (or period), allowing us to identify which timescales dominate the variability."
- p.38 (viewed):
$$y=\sin x=A\sin(2\pi ft+\phi)=A\sin(\omega t+\phi),\qquad y=\cos x=A\cos(2\pi ft+\phi)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$$
  A: amplitude; f: "frequency or number of oscillations per time steps"; ω: "radian frequency (=2πf)"; φ: "phase shift in radian"; t: "length of the time period of interest"; **period: 1/f**; **K: wavenumber, "(an integer) number of oscillations that fit inside the domain, k=ft"**. Figure: harmonics j=1,2,3 for N=24; $\omega_1=2\pi\cdot1/24=0.262$ rad/sample completes one cycle over N=24.
- p.40–41 harmonic decomposition:
$$X_t=A_o+\sum_{k=1}^{N/2}\left(A_j\cos(\omega_jt)+B_j\sin(\omega_jt)\right)$$
  "This decomposition can be applied to any time series: N data points → N+1 coefficients (A₀ + A_j,B_j pairs)" (see errata); "One can derive A_j and B_j through regression fit or more efficiently via the Fourier Transform"; "if N is even, j goes up to N/2, if N is odd, j goes up to (N−1)/2"; "This is **Nyquist wavenumber/frequency**, the highest frequency that can be resolved in your data using harmonic analysis." Figure: N=24 example components $3\cos(\omega_1t)+1\sin(\omega_1t)$, $1\cos(\omega_2t)-1.5\sin(\omega_2t)$, $0.5\cos(\omega_3t)+0.8\sin(\omega_3t)$, Nyquist term $1.2\cos(\pi t)$ ("j=N/2 component (Nyquist, no sine term)"); "Summing all harmonics j=1…N/2 exactly reproduces the original data".
- p.44–45 Hamburg Tmax (1950–): (1) fit seasonality; (2) start from appropriate k, derive $\hat X_t$, variance explained $\frac{var(\hat X_t)}{var(X_t)}\times100\%$; (3) repeatedly add k until satisfied. **var(X_t)=54.37, var($\hat X_t$)=49.61, variance explained = 0.9124 = 91.24%** (verified). Table: harmonic 1, period 12 (months), amplitude 9.53 °C, 91.24%; harmonic 2, period 6, 0.42, 0.18% (91.42); harmonic 3, period 4, 0.20, 0.04% (91.46). Harmonic 1: $-9.529\cos(2\pi t)-0.177\sin(2\pi t)$, amplitude 9.530 °C. "The 'k' here does not count cycles over the record. It counts cycles per unit of t, and we defined t in years." Residual = data − seasonal cycle (anomaly, warming visible).
- p.47–48 Precipitation: NYC monthly, K=1 explains 3.1% (seasonal max ~Jul–Aug); LA monthly K=2 32.7%; PRISM Oregon K=1 49.2%.
- p.49 "Climatology explains slightly more of the seasonal variance because it is equivalent to using all harmonics up to N/2"; "If the seasonal cycle is asymmetric (e.g., a slow warm-up and a fast cool-down), K = 1 cannot represent its shape; K = 2, 3, or higher is required"; "The seasonal cycle is a very good example for harmonic. It is deterministic, forced by insolation, phase-locked to the calendar, and repeats every year"; "What if we have a time series X without knowing which frequencies dominate?"
- p.50 Periodogram: "Power spectrum is just what happens when you stop hand-picking which frequencies to test (in the harmonic analysis) and instead compute the contribution of every resolvable frequency at once"; power at each k: $A_k^2+B_k^2$.
- p.51 Hamburg Tmax PSD (log): sharp peak at 1 cycle/yr (91.5% variance from annual cycle); raw periodogram + Welch (smoothed).
- p.52 NYC precipitation (3.3% from annual cycle): flat, no clear peak; PRISM Oregon (53.2% from K=1,2): peak at 1 cycle/yr, small at 2.
- p.53 Welch method steps: cut record into 10-yr segments (50% overlap), taper each (Hann window), periodogram of each, average at each frequency (25 segments) → smoother but coarser (61 bins, spacing 0.1 cycles/yr vs raw 787 bins).
- p.54 RONI power spectrum 1950–2026: broad peak in ENSO band 2–7 yr (0.14–0.5 cycles/yr), Welch 10-yr and 20-yr segments. Lecture08_example.ipynb.
- p.55 white noise (N=332, std=1): "no dominant frequency, scatters around a flat level" (theoretical flat spectrum 0.078).
- p.56–57 hands-on Unknown X (daily, 200 yr): Welch 40-yr segments; peaks ~45–48 days (intraseasonal, ~8 cycles/yr), ~4 yr, ~10 yr (see L09-10 p.5–8 for code and band-pass solution).
