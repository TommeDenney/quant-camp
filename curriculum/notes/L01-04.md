## 2. Moments, quantiles (L01-02 p.29–31, 43–45)

p.29 (viewed):
$$\hat\mu=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N x_n,\qquad \hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\hat\mu)^2,\qquad \hat\sigma=\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2}$$
- Skewness "measures asymmetry by looking at **cubed (thus preserve the sign)** deviations from the mean, standardized by σ³":
$$\hat\gamma=\frac{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\hat\mu)^3}{\hat\sigma^3}$$
  γ>0 "a longer right tail"; γ<0 "a longer left tail"; |γ̂|<0.5 approximately symmetric; 0.5≤|γ̂|<1 moderate skewed; |γ̂|≥1 highly skewed.
- Kurtosis "measures flatness or peakedness (extreme) by looking at the fourth power of deviations from the mean, standardized by σ⁴":
$$\hat\kappa=\frac{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^N (x_n-\hat\mu)^4}{\hat\sigma^4}$$
  κ̂>3 "heavier tailed (more, flatted than Gaussian)"; κ̂<3 "lighter tailed (more peaked than Gaussian)". (Pearson convention: normal = 3.)
p.30 invariance table (viewed): mean — shift No, scale No; variance — shift Yes, scale No; skewness — Yes, yes; kurtosis — Yes, yes. ("Scale-invariant (standardization)".)
p.31 Quantiles: "after the data are ordered … at least p·100% of the data are below that values; at least (1-p)% are above"; median = middle value; 95th percentile; ordered $x_{(1)}\le\dots\le x_{(N)}$; median $=x_{([N+1]/2)}$ (N odd), $(x_{([N]/2)}+x_{([N+1]/2)})/2$ (N even, as written). "The median (50th quantile) tells you the center. The interquantile range (e.g., 5th to 95th, or 25th to 75th) tells you spread"; symmetric if median-to-95th ≈ 5th-to-median.
p.43 Niño 3.4 hands-on: compute mean, variance, std, skewness, kurtosis and quantiles; "what moments/variables are sensitive to whether climatology is removed or not?" (answer by invariance table: removing a monthly climatology is not a constant shift, so all can change, but mean and variance change most.)
p.45 Summer daily Tmax non-normal (nonzero skewness and excess kurtosis), North America projections: shifts in mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis; "More consistent warming in daily minimum than daily maximum"; "Decrease in variance except in the Texas"; "Large increase in skewness in the NE".
Python: numpy mean/var(ddof)/std, scipy.stats.skew, scipy.stats.kurtosis, np.quantile/percentile (L03-04 p.4 review list).

## 4. Correlation (L01-02 p.62–63; L03-04 p.9–14, 31–32, 38)

- Pearson "assumes linearity"; Spearman "captures monotonic-but-nonlinear relationships".
$$r=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_x\sigma_y},\quad \mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N(x_i-\hat x)(y_i-\hat y),\qquad \rho=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R(X),R(Y))}{\sigma_{R(x)}\sigma_{R(y)}}$$
- L03-04 p.9 example: Pearson = +0.851, Spearman = +1 for a monotonic curve.
- p.63/p.11 example: Spearman −0.306 (p=0.0076) vs Pearson −0.252 (p=0.029): "Spearman is noticeably stronger … the monotonic relationship is stronger than the linear one, consistent with the physical mechanism". Functions: `scipy.stats.pearsonr(x,y)`, `scipy.stats.spearmanr` (slide writes "spearman").
- p.12–14 Pattern correlations for model evaluation; tropical Pacific trend inconsistency (El Niño-like vs La Niña-like).
- Correlation test (L03-04 p.31):
$$\hat\rho=\frac{\sum (x_j-\hat\mu_x)(y_j-\hat\mu_y)}{\sqrt{\sum (x_j-\hat\mu_x)^2\sum (y_k-\hat\mu_y)^2}},\qquad T=\frac{\hat\rho-\rho}{SE(\hat\rho)}=(\hat\rho-\rho)\sqrt{\frac{N-2}{1-\hat\rho^2}}\sim t_{N-2}$$
  "If X and Y are independent, then their correlation coefficient vanishes. Conversely, if the correlation coefficient does not vanish, then X and Y are not independent."
- p.32 example: H0 ρ=0; $T=\frac{0.048\sqrt{100-2}}{\sqrt{1-(-0.048)^2}}=-0.484$ (sic); figure: r=0.049, df=98, t=0.484, p=0.630 → "not able to reject H0".
- p.38 Niño 3.4 early vs recent "correlation: 0.12, p = 0.02478 … Does this test make sense??" — teaching point: correlating two different periods month-by-month has no physical pairing; the test is meaningless even if "significant".

## 5. Hypothesis testing (L03-04 p.15–41; review p.5–8)

- p.15 motivating question: heatwave distribution shifted at α=0.05 — "What is the chance of wrongly rejecting a true null (no shift) hypothesis" (= α).
- p.17 parametric tests: infer distribution parameters; most common: same mean → t-test; same variance → F-test; independent → correlation test.
- p.18 α: "The chances of wrongly reject the hypothesis (H0) when H0 is actually true"; "How much you are willing to be wrong"; "A decision rule". Figure: two-tailed critical 1.96.
- p.19 p-value: "the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you actually got" / "how surprising is what I saw?"; "α fixes the boundary of the red zone in advance — … outer 2.5% (two tails)"; "p-value is the actual area beyond your observed test statistic, computed after the fact"; p<α → reject. Examples p = 0.005, 0.02, 0.06.
- t-test (p.22–25): assume σx=σy; H0 μx=μy, Ha μx≠μy. If Gaussian, $\hat\mu_x-\hat\mu_y\sim N(\mu_x-\mu_y,\sigma_x^2/N_x+\sigma_y^2/N_y)$ ("can be derived … beyond the scope"). Z-value $=(\bar x-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt N)$; denominator = **Standard Error**. Pooled: $T=\frac{(\hat\mu_x-\hat\mu_y)-(\mu_x-\mu_y)}{\sqrt{1/N_x+1/N_y}\,\hat\sigma_p}$, df = Nx+Ny−2.
  - p.24 under H0: Student pooled $T_0=\frac{\hat\mu_x-\hat\mu_y}{\sqrt{1/N_x+1/N_y}\sqrt{\frac{(N_x-1)\hat\sigma_x^2+(N_x-1)\hat\sigma_x^2}{N_x+N_y-2}}}$ (sic) vs Welch $T_0=\frac{\hat\mu_x-\hat\mu_y}{\sqrt{\hat\sigma_x^2/N_x+\hat\sigma_y^2/N_y}}$, Welch–Satterthwaite $df=\frac{(\hat\sigma_x^2/N_x+\hat\sigma_y^2/N_y)^2}{\frac{(\hat\sigma_x^2/N_x)^2}{N_x-1}+\frac{(\hat\sigma_y^2/N_y)^2}{N_y-1}}$. "Welch's: each group's variance is used separately"; "Student's: … single pooled variance … assumes equal variances – easier to lead wrongly reject H0, and not robust with unequal sample size"; "Python function use Welch's by default" (see errata).
  - p.25 worked: μ̂x=50.0, μ̂y=50.60, σ̂x²=4, σ̂y²=6.5, N=100 each; $\sqrt{4/100+6.5/100}\approx0.32$; $t_0=(50-50.6)/0.32=-1.85$ (verified −1.852); figure "Welch's t-test df=187.1, p=0.066" (verified p=0.0659) → "not able to reject H0".
- p.26 t vs z: z when true σ² known; t when σ² estimated ("adds an extra layer of uncertainty"); t: mean 0, variance depends on sample size, N→∞ → N(0,1). Figure df=1,3,10,30,700.
- F-test (p.28–30): H0 σx=σy; $\frac{(N-1)\hat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{N-1}$;
$$F=\frac{\frac{(N_x-1)\hat\sigma_x^2}{\sigma_x^2}/(N_x-1)}{\frac{(N_y-1)\hat\sigma_y^2}{\sigma_y^2}/(N_y-1)}=\frac{\hat\sigma_x^2}{\hat\sigma_y^2}\frac{\sigma_y^2}{\sigma_x^2}\sim F_{(N_x-1),(N_y-1)};\quad F_0=\frac{\hat\sigma_x^2}{\hat\sigma_y^2}\ \text{under }H_0$$
  "A significant difference in variance corresponding to as value of F different from one." p.30: $\hat F=6.5/4=1.64$ (sic, = 1.625) → "able to reject H0 … distinguishable variance" (verified two-sided p=0.0165 with df 99,99).
- p.33 "As test of variance doesn't require assumption of same mean, one can always directly to test variance first."
- p.34 notes: "A test statistic is a function of one or more random variables that does not depend on any unknown parameters"; t-test assumes the sampling distribution of the mean is normal; F-test sensitive to non-normality; CLT — how large is large enough? test normality or plot histograms vs sample size.
- p.35–38 Niño 3.4 1951–1980 vs 1996–2025: n=360 each; means 26.74 / 27.0; std 0.91 / 1.023; Welch t = 3.48, p = 0.000527 → mean shift significant; F = 1.5859, p = 1.383e−05 → variance change (**later corrected**: F = 1.2593, see L07-08 p.5); correlation 0.12, p=0.02478 "Does this test make sense??".
- p.39–40 PRISM Oregon hands-on: annual mean temp / precipitation mean & variance change, correlation.
- Python (L05-06 slide 12 review list): compute critical values and p-values with ppf and cdf; scipy tests; "Write a classic F-test" (scipy has no classical F-test function — review table says "None for Classical F-test"); permutation; bootstrap.

### Non-parametric (L03-04 p.42–68)
- p.43 parametric = prescribed family with parameters; non-parametric = "distribution-free and make fewer assumptions"; work on ranks. Tests: Wilcoxon rank-sum, Kruskal–Wallis, KS, Spearman.
- Wilcoxon rank-sum (p.44–52): "Non-parametric version of t-test" but "doesn't test means at all … only keeps the order"; H0: P(X>Y)=0.5 — "a statement about the whole distribution"; detects systematic shift even when means similar.
  - Idealized Nx=Ny=3 (p.45–47, viewed): example Rx=7; all 20 combinations listed (6…15); P(Rx≤6 or ≥15)=2/20=10%; ≤7/≥14 = 20%; ≤8/≥13 = 40%; ≤9/≥12 = 70%; ≤10/≥11 = 100%. At α=0.05 can't reject.
  - p.48 number of arrangements $\binom{N}{N_x}=\frac{N!}{(N-N_x)!N_x!}$; $\binom63=20$.
  - p.49: $R_x=\sum_{i=1}^{N_x}\mathrm{rank}(X_i)$; $E[R_x]=\frac{N_x(N_x+N_y+1)}{2}$; $\mathrm{Var}[R_x]=\frac{N_xN_y(N_x+N_y+1)}{12}$; $z=\frac{R_x-E[R_x]}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[R_x]}}\sim N(0,1)$ (Gaussian for sufficiently large N).
  - p.50 worked: Rx=15 (most extreme); E=10.5; Var=5.25; "z_cor = 1.74 → p-value 0.81" then "fail to reject H0 since p=0.081>0.05" (verified: continuity-corrected z=(15−10.5−0.5)/√5.25=1.746, p=0.081).
  - p.52 stochastic dominance: Y dominates X if P(Y>z)>P(X>z) ∀z, i.e. $F_Y(z)<F_X(z)$ ∀z.
- Kruskal–Wallis (p.53–58, viewed): "extends the Wilcoxon rank-sum test from two groups to more than two — it's the nonparametric version of analysis of Variance". E experiments × C conditions; $R_c=\sum_{e=1}^E\mathrm{rank}[Y_{ec}]$;
$$\chi^2=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}[R_c]}\sum_{c=1}^C(R_c-E[R_c])^2\sim\chi^2_{C-1},\quad E[R_c]=\frac{E(E\times C+1)}{2},\quad \mathrm{Var}[R_c]\approx\frac{E(E\times C)(E\times C+1)}{12}$$
  (large-N approx (N−1)≈N, (N−E)≈N). "large spread → large χ² → reject H0."
  - Worked: 15 years of storm intensity, basins A,B,C; H0 $F_A(z)\approx F_B(z)\approx F_C(z)$; rank sums 211, 396, 428; E=15(46)/2=345.0; Var=15(45)(46)/12=2587.5; χ²=10.67 (sic; verified 10.607, p=0.005, crit 5.99, df=2) → "Reject H0"; p.58 "Basin A's intensity is statistically dominated by Basins B and C".
- KS (p.59–61): compares "entire distributions"; ECDF $F_N(x)=\frac1N\sum\mathbb 1(x_i\le x)$; $D=\max_z|F_X(z)-F_Y(z)|$; $K=\sqrt{\frac{N_xN_y}{N_x+N_y}}D$ ~ Kolmogorov distribution; H0 $F_X(z)=F_Y(z)$. Basin A vs C: D=0.667, p=0.0018 → different; λ=1.826, asymptotic P=0.0025 (verified). Basin B vs C: similar.
- p.62–64 DJF Niño 3.4 1951–1980 vs 1996–2025: Wilcoxon "z = −0.47 → p-value 0.94"; "fail to reject"; KS D=0.128, p=0.9278, λ=0.49.
- p.65–67 Oregon precip/temperature by season: KS for AMJ vs OND (slides also say JMA), Kruskal–Wallis for all seasons.

### Errors, FDR, resampling (L03-04 p.69–78; review p.7–8; L05-06 slides 8–11)
- p.69 "When you reject a H0 under significance level of 0.05, what is the chance you are being wrong".
- p.70 / review p.8 table (DelSole & Tippett Fig 4.5): Accept H0 when H0 true = correct; Accept H0 when H0 false = **Type II error, probability β**; Reject H0 when true = **Type I error, probability α**; Reject when false = correct, **power = 1−β**. L05-06 slide 12: "Understand Type I and Type II errors and how to reduce them."
- p.71–72 NASA subseasonal TC ensemble (atmosphere vs ocean perturbation); 78 black dots "statistically significant"; "differences are small … but significant in some areas".
- p.73 FDR: "If you run 100 tests at the usual 5% significance level, you'd expect about 5 of them to falsely show up as significant"; rank p-values $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(N)}$; "scale the p-values (multiple by N/i) or find new threshold" (Benjamini–Hochberg).
- p.74 result: raw p<0.05: 137; FDR significant: 0; min raw p 0.000922; min FDR p 0.649; code `reject, p_corrected = fdrcorrection(p_raw ,alpha=0.5, method="indep")` (sic).
- Permutation (p.75; L05-06 s.8): "Shuffling and regrouping"; H0: X and Y are **exchangeable** (from same distribution); relabel all items into X_new, Y_new; recompute statistic each time to build a distribution; see where the actual observed statistic falls. ENSO DJF, Wilcoxon z two-tailed: observed z=−0.067, p(normal)=0.947, p(permutation)=0.934.
- Bootstrap (p.76; L05-06 s.9): "Instead of testing a hypothesis, bootstrap estimates the uncertainty (e.g., a confidence interval) of a statistic, when you don't know its sampling distribution analytically"; "resample **with replacement** from your single observed sample (same size as original), recompute the statistic, repeat many times". Figure: DJF Niño 3.4 diff in means (1996–2025 − 1951–1980), 1000 resamples, observed diff 0.096, 95% CI [−0.415, 0.642]; text: "Since the 95% confidence interval comfortably contains zero … no strong evidence of a real difference … the observed −0.0624 difference (sic) is easily consistent with pure sampling noise."
- p.78 / L05-06 s.11: "Hypothesis test directly answers 'is there a difference'"; "Bootstrap answers 'how much uncertainty is there around my estimate of the difference?'". How to choose: compare specific properties (mean, variance) → t-test, F-test, permutation, bootstrap; ask whether samples come from the same distribution → most tests if assumptions met; plenty of computing power → permutation & bootstrap.
